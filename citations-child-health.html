Brain Development & Cognition

Hutton, J.S., Dudley, J., Horowitz-Kraus, T., DeWitt, T., & Holland, S.K. (2020). Associations between screen-based media use and brain white matter integrity in preschool-aged children. JAMA Pediatrics, 174(1), e193869. doi:10.1001/jamapediatrics.2019.3869

Key findingFirst MRI study of its kind: preschoolers with screen use above AAP guidelines showed lower structural integrity of white matter tracts supporting language, executive function, and emergent literacy, plus lower scores on language and literacy measures.

jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/2754101

Hutton, J.S., Piotrowski, J.T., Bagot, K., et al. (2022). Associations between digital media use and brain surface structural measures in preschool-aged children. Scientific Reports, 12, 19095. doi:10.1038/s41598-022-20922-0

Key findingHigher screen use in 3- to 5-year-olds was associated with lower cortical thickness and sulcal depth in brain regions supporting visual processing, attention, memory encoding, letter recognition, and social cognition.

nature.com/articles/s41598-022-20922-0

Hutton, J.S., Dudley, J., Horowitz-Kraus, T., DeWitt, T., & Holland, S.K. (2020). Differences in functional brain network connectivity during stories presented in audio, illustrated, and animated format in preschool-age children. Brain Imaging and Behavior, 14(1), 130–141. doi:10.1007/s11682-018-9985-y

Key findingBrain network connectivity supporting language and imagery was strongest and most balanced during illustrated storybook reading compared to animated (screen) formats.

Horowitz-Kraus, T., & Hutton, J.S. (2018). Brain connectivity in children is increased by the time they spend reading books and decreased by the length of exposure to screen-based media. Acta Paediatrica, 107(4), 685–693. doi:10.1111/apa.14176

Key findingBook reading time was associated with increased brain connectivity in children; screen time was associated with decreased connectivity in the same networks.

Jomaa, F., Ebraheem, F., & Horowitz-Kraus, T. (2025). Greater parent–child brain synchronisation during printed book versus screen reading using hyperscanning electroencephalograph data. Acta Paediatrica, 114(7), 1633–1641. doi:10.1111/apa.70007

Key findingParent–child neural synchrony — a marker of attuned, relational learning — was greater during printed book reading than screen reading.

Takeuchi, H., Taki, Y., Asano, K., Asano, M., Sassa, Y., Yokota, S., Kotozaki, Y., Nouchi, R., & Kawashima, R. (2018). Impact of frequency of internet use on development of brain structures and verbal intelligence: Longitudinal analyses. Human Brain Mapping, 39(11), 4471–4479. doi:10.1002/hbm.24286

Key findingIn a longitudinal sample of Japanese children, higher frequency of internet use was associated with smaller increases in gray and white matter volume across brain regions (including prefrontal and anterior cingulate cortex) and lower verbal intelligence development over time.
02

Handwriting vs. Typing

Van der Weel, F.R., & Van der Meer, A.L.H. (2024). Handwriting but not typewriting leads to widespread brain connectivity: A high-density EEG study with implications for the classroom. Frontiers in Psychology, 14, 1219945. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1219945

Key finding256-channel EEG showed handwriting produced far more elaborate, widespread theta/alpha brain connectivity patterns in regions crucial for memory formation and learning than typing; the authors conclude handwriting promotes learning in ways keyboarding does not.

frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1219945/full

Ose Askvik, E., Van der Weel, F.R., & Van der Meer, A.L.H. (2020). The importance of cursive handwriting over typewriting for learning in the classroom: A high-density EEG study of 12-year-old children and young adults. Frontiers in Psychology, 11, 1810. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2020.01810

Key findingThe same handwriting-superior connectivity pattern was found in 12-year-old schoolchildren as in adults, with cursive producing the widest connectivity, directly relevant to K-12 policy.

frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.01810/full

Mueller, P.A., & Oppenheimer, D.M. (2014). The pen is mightier than the keyboard: Advantages of longhand over laptop note taking. Psychological Science, 25(6), 1159–1168. doi:10.1177/0956797614524581

Key findingAcross three experiments, laptop note-takers performed worse on conceptual questions; typing encourages shallow verbatim transcription rather than processing and reframing in one's own words. Evidence note: a 2019 direct replication by Morehead, Dunlosky & Rawson found smaller, non-significant effects favoring longhand.

Donica, D.K., Giroux, P., & Faust, A. (2018). Keyboarding instruction: Comparison of techniques for improved keyboarding skills in elementary students. Journal of Occupational Therapy, Schools, & Early Intervention, 11(4), 396–410. doi:10.1080/19411243.2018.1512067

03

Reading: Paper vs. Screens

Delgado, P., Vargas, C., Ackerman, R., & Salmerón, L. (2018). Don't throw away your printed books: A meta-analysis on the effects of reading media on reading comprehension. Educational Research Review, 25, 23–38. doi:10.1016/j.edurev.2018.09.003

Key findingMeta-analysis of 54 studies (170,000+ participants): paper reading produced reliably better comprehension than screens, especially for informational texts and under time pressure — and the screen disadvantage has grown, not shrunk, in studies of "digital native" cohorts.

Clinton, V. (2019). Reading from paper compared to screens: A systematic review and meta-analysis. Journal of Research in Reading, 42(2), 288–325. doi:10.1111/1467-9817.12269

Key findingIndependent meta-analysis confirming the paper advantage for comprehension of informational text, and finding readers on screens are more overconfident (poorer metacognitive calibration).

Kong, Y., Seo, Y.S., & Zhai, L. (2018). Comparison of reading performance on screen and on paper: A meta-analysis. Computers & Education, 123, 138–149. doi:10.1016/j.compedu.2018.05.005

Key findingA third converging meta-analysis: reading comprehension was better on paper, with no time savings from screens.

Furenes, M.I., Kucirkova, N., & Bus, A.G. (2021). A comparison of children's reading on paper versus screen: A meta-analysis. Review of Educational Research, 91(4), 483–517. doi:10.3102/0034654321998074

Key findingFocused on young children's shared book reading: poorly designed digital enhancements impede comprehension, while adult mediation during print reading beats children reading digital books alone.

Columbia University, Teachers College (May 2024). "Children derive deeper meaning from printed texts than screens, according to new brain study from Teachers College" (neuroimaging study by the Froud lab finding deeper semantic processing of print in middle schoolers).

Key findingPair this press item with the underlying peer-reviewed article when citing formally; the meta-analyses above (Delgado, Clinton, Kong, Furenes) are the strongest citable anchors for this bucket.

tc.columbia.edu — press release

04

Attention, Distraction & ADHD-Related Symptoms

Ra, C.K., Cho, J., Stone, M.D., et al. (2018). Association of digital media use with subsequent symptoms of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder among adolescents. JAMA, 320(3), 255–263. doi:10.1001/jama.2018.8931

Key finding24-month longitudinal cohort of ~2,600 teens with no baseline ADHD symptoms: high-frequency digital media use predicted higher odds of developing ADHD symptoms over the following two years.

Thorell, L.B., Burén, J., Ström Wiman, J., Sandberg, D., & Nutley, S.B. (2024). Longitudinal associations between digital media use and ADHD symptoms in children and adolescents: A systematic literature review. European Child & Adolescent Psychiatry, 33, 2503–2526. doi:10.1007/s00787-022-02130-3

Key findingKarolinska Institutet review of 28 longitudinal studies: support for reciprocal effects. Digital media use (especially problematic use) contributes to later ADHD symptom levels, partly via effects on sleep and social relations.

Sana, F., Weston, T., & Cepeda, N.J. (2013). Laptop multitasking hinders classroom learning for both users and nearby peers. Computers & Education, 62, 24–31. doi:10.1016/j.compedu.2012.10.003

Key findingIn-class laptop multitasking lowered test scores not only for the user but for surrounding students who could see the screen — a secondhand-distraction effect directly relevant to 1:1 device classrooms.

OECD (2023). PISA 2022 Results (Volume II): Learning During — and From — Disruption; and OECD (2024), Students, Digital Devices and Success.

Key findingAcross OECD countries, ~two-thirds of 15-year-olds report being distracted by digital devices in math class; distraction by other students' devices is associated with a performance gap equivalent to roughly three-quarters of a year of learning; students spending 5–7 hours/day on devices at school scored 49 points lower in math than light users after socioeconomic adjustment.

oecd.org — Students, Digital Devices and Success

05

EdTech & Device Programs: Academic Outcomes

Lee, J., et al. (2022). Exploring the negative and gap-widening effects of EdTech on young children's learning achievement: Evidence from a longitudinal dataset of children in American K–3 classrooms (ECLS-K:2011). International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, 19(9), 5430.

Key findingNationally representative U.S. longitudinal data: EdTech use in K–3 showed a negative effect on learning achievement and a gap-widening effect, with harms concentrated among already-disadvantaged students.

ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9104322

Mora, T., Escardíbul, J.-O., & Di Pietro, G. (2018). Computers and students' achievement: An analysis of the One Laptop per Child program in Catalonia. International Journal of Educational Research, 92, 145–157. doi:10.1016/j.ijer.2018.09.013

Key findingPopulation-level longitudinal data (2009–2016): the 1:1 laptop program reduced student performance in Catalan, Spanish, English, and mathematics by 0.20–0.22 SD, with larger harms for boys.

Vigdor, J.L., Ladd, H.F., & Martinez, E. (2014). Scaling the digital divide: Home computer technology and student achievement. Economic Inquiry, 52(3), 1103–1119. doi:10.1111/ecin.12089

Key findingNorth Carolina administrative data on ~1 million students: expanded home computer and broadband access led to modest but persistent declines in reading and math scores, with larger declines for disadvantaged students.

Malamud, O., & Pop-Eleches, C. (2011). Home computer use and the development of human capital. Quarterly Journal of Economics, 126(2), 987–1027. doi:10.1093/qje/qjr008

Key findingRegression-discontinuity evidence from a voucher program: children who won home computers had significantly lower school grades in math, English, and Romanian.

Cellphone policy

Beland, L.-P., & Murphy, R. (2016). Ill communication: Technology, distraction & student performance. Labour Economics, 41, 61–76. sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0927537116300136

Key findingStudy of 91 schools across four English cities before and after banning mobile phones: test scores for 16-year-olds rose 6.4%. Gains were concentrated among low-achieving and low-income students (14.2% of a SD), with no significant effect on high achievers — suggesting a phone ban is a low-cost way to narrow achievement gaps, not just raise averages.

cep.lse.ac.uk — LSE Centre for Economic Performance working paper

Figlio, D.N., & Özek, U. (2025). The Impact of Cellphone Bans in Schools on Student Outcomes: Evidence from Florida. NBER Working Paper No. 34388. nber.org/papers/w34388

Key findingOne of the ten largest U.S. school districts (Florida) required phones silenced and stored during the school day. Suspensions rose sharply in year one — especially among Black students — but faded after that. Test scores improved starting in year two, driven largely by reduced unexcused absences; effects were larger in middle/high school and among boys.

Mental Health

Twenge, J.M., & Campbell, W.K. (2018). Associations between screen time and lower psychological well-being among children and adolescents: Evidence from a population-based study. Preventive Medicine Reports, 12, 271–283. doi:10.1016/j.pmedr.2018.10.003

Key findingN > 40,000 U.S. children/adolescents: high screen users (7+ hrs/day) were more than twice as likely to have been diagnosed with depression or anxiety, and showed lower self-control and emotional stability, versus low users; associations were larger for adolescents than young children.

Boers, E., Afzali, M.H., Newton, N., & Conrod, P. (2019). Association of screen time and depression in adolescence. JAMA Pediatrics, 173(9), 853–859. doi:10.1001/jamapediatrics.2019.1759

Key finding4-year longitudinal study of ~3,800 adolescents: within-person increases in social media and television use predicted increases in depressive symptoms, consistent with upward social comparison and displacement mechanisms.

Shannon, H., Bush, K., Villeneuve, P.J., Hellemans, K.G., & Guimond, S. (2022). Problematic social media use in adolescents and young adults: Systematic review and meta-analysis. JMIR Mental Health, 9(4), e33450. doi:10.2196/33450

Key findingMeta-analysis of 18 studies (N = 9,269): problematic social media use was moderately and significantly correlated with depression (r = .27), anxiety (r = .35), and stress (r = .31).

Liu, M., Kamper-DeMarco, K.E., Zhang, J., Xiao, J., Dong, D., & Xue, P. (2022). Time spent on social media and risk of depression in adolescents: A dose-response meta-analysis. International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, 19(9), 5164. doi:10.3390/ijerph19095164

Key findingPooled analysis of 26 studies (N = 55,340): risk of depression rose with each additional hour of daily social media time in a dose-response pattern.

ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9103874

Li, L., Zhang, Q., Zhu, L., Zeng, G., Huang, H., et al. (2022). Screen time and depression risk: A meta-analysis of cohort studies. Frontiers in Psychiatry, 13, 1058572. doi:10.3389/fpsyt.2022.1058572

Key findingProspective cohort studies only: higher screen time predicted elevated subsequent depression risk.
07

Physical Health: Sleep, Vision, Sedentary Harms

Carter, B., Rees, P., Hale, L., Bhattacharjee, D., & Paradkar, M.S. (2016). Association between portable screen-based media device access or use and sleep outcomes: A systematic review and meta-analysis. JAMA Pediatrics, 170(12), 1202–1208. doi:10.1001/jamapediatrics.2016.2341

Key findingChildren 6–19 with bedtime device access had roughly double the odds of inadequate sleep quantity, poor sleep quality, and excessive daytime sleepiness (OR 2.27) — even mere access without use was harmful.

Hale, L., & Guan, S. (2015). Screen time and sleep among school-aged children and adolescents: A systematic literature review. Sleep Medicine Reviews, 21, 50–58. doi:10.1016/j.smrv.2014.07.007

Key finding90% of included studies found screen time adversely associated with sleep outcomes (shortened duration, delayed timing) in school-aged children and adolescents.

Lanca, C., & Saw, S.-M. (2020). The association between digital screen time and myopia: A systematic review. Ophthalmic and Physiological Optics, 40(2), 216–229. doi:10.1111/opo.12657

Zong, Z., et al. (2024). The association between screen time exposure and myopia in children and adolescents: A meta-analysis. BMC Public Health, 24, 1625. doi:10.1186/s12889-024-19113-5

Key findingPooled evidence across 19 studies linking higher screen exposure to elevated myopia risk in children and adolescents; complements a 2025 BMC dose-response meta-analysis (766,848 participants aged 5–19) finding sedentary screen/near-work behavior raises myopia risk while outdoor time is protective (doi:10.1186/s12889-025-22434-8).

bmcpublichealth.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12889-024-19113-5

Fang, K., Mu, M., Liu, K., & He, Y. (2019). Screen time and childhood overweight/obesity: A systematic review and meta-analysis. Child: Care, Health and Development, 45(5), 744–753. doi:10.1111/cch.12701

Key findingHigher screen time was significantly associated with childhood overweight/obesity risk.

Janssen, X., Martin, A., Hughes, A.R., Hill, C.M., Kotronoulas, G., & Hesketh, K.R. (2020). Associations of screen time, sedentary time and physical activity with sleep in under 5s: A systematic review and meta-analysis. Sleep Medicine Reviews, 49, 101226. doi:10.1016/j.smrv.2019.101226

Key findingScreen time is associated with poorer sleep outcomes beginning in infancy and toddlerhood; physical activity and outdoor play are favorably associated with sleep, supporting analog/outdoor defaults in education.

Social-Emotional Development & Human Connection

Uhls, Y.T., Michikyan, M., Morris, J., Garcia, D., Small, G.W., Zgourou, E., & Greenfield, P.M. (2014). Five days at outdoor education camp without screens improves preteen skills with nonverbal emotion cues. Computers in Human Behavior, 39, 387–392. doi:10.1016/j.chb.2014.05.036

Key findingField experiment with 105 sixth-graders: just five screen-free days at an outdoor education camp significantly improved recognition of nonverbal emotional cues relative to matched controls who kept usual media use.

Hutton et al. (2022) — see Section 1.

Key findingCortical thinning associated with higher screen use included regions involved in social cognition, reasoning, and empathy — areas that should be thickening at preschool age.

Center for Democracy & Technology (Oct 2025). CDT survey research finds use of AI in K-12 schools connected to negative effects on students, including their real-life relationships.

cdt.org — Off Task report

Grundmeier, R.W., et al. (2026). Generative artificial intelligence: Implications for families and pediatricians. Pediatrics. doi:10.1542/peds.2025-074912

Key findingAAP review concluding generative-AI risks to children are most acute in early and middle childhood.

Addictive & Persuasive Design ("Dark Patterns")

Radesky, J., Hiniker, A., McLaren, C., Akgun, E., Schaller, A., Weeks, H.M., Campbell, S., & Gearhardt, A.N. (2022). Prevalence and characteristics of manipulative design in mobile applications used by children. JAMA Network Open, 5(6), e2217641. doi:10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2022.17641

Key findingOf apps used by 160 children aged 3–5, 80% contained manipulative design (parasocial pressure, fabricated time pressure, navigation constraints, lures); nearly 99% of children had at least one such app, with manipulative design most prevalent in apps used by lower-SES children.

Meyer, M., Zosh, J.M., McLaren, C., Robb, M., McCaffery, H., Golinkoff, R.M., Hirsh-Pasek, K., & Radesky, J. (2021). How educational are "educational" apps for young children? App store content analysis using the Four Pillars of Learning framework. Journal of Children and Media, 15(4), 526–548. doi:10.1080/17482798.2021.1882516

Key findingMost top "educational" apps scored low on established science-of-learning criteria. Marketing claims of educational value are largely unsubstantiated.

Zhao, F., Egelman, S., Weeks, H.M., Kaciroti, N., Miller, A.L., & Radesky, J.S. (2020). Data collection practices of mobile applications played by preschool-aged children. JAMA Pediatrics, 174(12), e203345. doi:10.1001/jamapediatrics.2020.3345

Key findingApps played by preschoolers showed high frequencies of persistent-identifier transmission to third parties, suggesting COPPA-relevant privacy rules are not being enforced.

Kidron, B., Evans, A., & Afia, J. (2018). Disrupted Childhood: The Cost of Persuasive Design. 5Rights Foundation.

Key findingFoundational policy report documenting how persuasive design strategies (variable rewards, infinite scroll, social validation loops) are deployed against developmental vulnerabilities of children.
10

Expert Consensus, Policy Statements & International Rollbacks

Karolinska Institutet (2023). Statement on Sweden's national digitalisation strategy in education.

Key finding"There's clear scientific evidence that digital tools impair rather than enhance student learning," recommending a return to printed textbooks and teacher expertise. Sweden subsequently reversed mandatory digital devices in preschools, ended digital learning for children under 6, and funded a national return to physical textbooks following PIRLS reading declines (2016–2021).

apnews.com — Sweden digital education backlash

UNESCO (2023). Global Education Monitoring Report 2023: Technology in education — A tool on whose terms?

Key findingUNESCO's "urgent call for appropriate use of technology in education": technology should never replace in-person, teacher-led instruction; finds little robust evidence of added educational value for much classroom technology, with much of the favorable evidence funded by the companies selling the products.

unesco.org/gem-report/en/technology

American Academy of Pediatrics (2025). Digital ecosystems, children, and adolescents. Robert W. Grundmeier et al., "Generative Artificial Intelligence: Implications for Families and Pediatricians," Pediatrics, published March 4, 2026. DOI: 10.1542/peds.2025-074912

Key findingThe review synthesizes current evidence on risks of generative AI for children, emphasizing that risks vary by developmental stage and are most acute in early and middle childhood.

Handbook of Children and Screens (2025). Christakis, D.A., & Hale, L. (Eds.), Handbook of Children and Screens: Digital Media, Development, and Well-Being from Birth Through Adolescence. Springer.

Key findingOpen-access synthesis of the full evidence base by 100+ researchers.

link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-031-69362-5

Common Sense Media, Youth AI Safety Institute (Jan. 2026). AI Risk Assessment: Google Search's AI Overview & AI Mode.

Key findingRated "Unacceptable" — the assessment's worst rating. Testing found the features missed clear signs of suicidal ideation and sometimes responded in ways that made things worse, validated disordered eating and paranoid/delusional thinking, and completed all 180 tested homework assignments outright rather than supporting learning. Because these features are built into Search itself rather than a separate product, schools and parents cannot disable them without blocking Google Search entirely. This is the source behind this site's own reference to Google's AI Mode being "rated Unacceptable Risk."

commonsensemedia.org — press release

institute.commonsensemedia.org — full risk assessment

Racism, Bias & Equity

"Equity" is a common rationale offered for expanding 1:1 devices, edtech platforms, and AI in classrooms: closing the "digital divide." The evidence below shows the opposite pattern: screen-heavy defaults concentrate their harms on Black, Hispanic, low-income, English-learner, disabled, and LGBTQ+ students, through four distinct mechanisms — (a) achievement gap-widening, (b) discriminatory surveillance and discipline, (c) algorithmic bias built into the tools themselves, and (d) exploitative design targeted at disadvantaged children.

a. Gap-Widening Academic Effects

Lee, J., et al. (2022) — cross-reference Section 5. Exploring the negative and gap-widening effects of EdTech on young children's learning achievement (ECLS-K:2011). International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, 19(9), 5430.

Key findingNationally representative U.S. K–3 data identified both a negative effect and a gap-widening effect: EdTech harms were concentrated among already-disadvantaged children, widening achievement gaps rather than closing them.

ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9104322

Vigdor, J.L., Ladd, H.F., & Martinez, E. (2014) — cross-reference Section 5. Scaling the digital divide: Home computer technology and student achievement. Economic Inquiry, 52(3), 1103–1119. doi:10.1111/ecin.12089

Key findingExpanded computer/broadband access produced persistent declines in reading and math, with the largest declines among disadvantaged students — direct evidence that device access alone widens, not narrows, socioeconomic achievement gaps.

Reich, J. (2020). Failure to Disrupt: Why Technology Alone Can't Transform Education. Harvard University Press.

Key findingSynthesizes decades of evidence on the "EdTech Matthew Effect": free and scaled learning technologies are disproportionately used and benefited from by already-advantaged learners, so technology-first reforms tend to reproduce and amplify existing inequality.

OECD (2023) — cross-reference Section 4. PISA 2022, Volume II.

Key findingDigital distraction harms are not evenly distributed: distraction and leisure device use at school are associated with larger performance losses among lower-SES students, compounding the gaps above.

b. Discriminatory Surveillance, Filtering & Discipline

Center for Democracy & Technology (Sept 2023). Off Task: EdTech Threats to Student Privacy and Equity in the Age of AI.

Key findingSchools' filtering software disproportionately blocks LGBTQ+ and race-related content (with Title I and special-education teachers more likely to report it), and student activity monitoring continues to produce discipline, outing, and law-enforcement contact that falls hardest on at-risk student groups.

cdt.org — Off Task report

Center for Democracy & Technology (Aug 2022). Hidden Harms: The Misleading Promise of Monitoring Students Online; and CDT research brief, Hidden Harms: Disproportionate Disciplinary Action (Nov 2022).

Key findingSurveys of students, parents, and teachers: monitoring software on school-issued devices is used more for discipline than safety. 48% of Black students and 55% of Hispanic students reported they or someone they knew got in trouble through monitoring, versus 41% of white students; 29% of LGBTQ+ students reported nonconsensual outing of sexual orientation or gender identity, and LGBTQ+ students were contacted by police at substantially higher rates (31% vs. 19%).

cdt.org — Hidden Harms report

Laird, E., et al. / Center for Democracy & Technology (2025). Beyond the Screen: Parents' Experiences with Student Activity Monitoring in K-12 Schools.

Key findingFollow-up parent research confirming Black, Hispanic, and LGBTQ+ students continue to report disproportionate harm from school device monitoring.

cdt.org — Beyond the Screen report

c. Algorithmic Bias Built Into Classroom Tools

Koenecke, A., Nam, A., Lake, E., Nudell, J., Quartey, M., Mengesha, Z., Toups, C., Rickford, J.R., Jurafsky, D., & Goel, S. (2020). Racial disparities in automated speech recognition. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 117(14), 7684–7689. doi:10.1073/pnas.1915768117

Key findingAll five major commercial speech-recognition systems (Amazon, Apple, Google, IBM, Microsoft) made roughly twice as many errors transcribing Black speakers as white speakers (word error rate 0.35 vs. 0.19) — the engines underlying voice-based reading tutors, literacy assessment, and assistive edtech systematically mis-hear Black students.

Liang, W., Yuksekgonul, M., Mao, Y., Wu, E., & Zou, J. (2023). GPT detectors are biased against non-native English writers. Patterns, 4(7), 100779. doi:10.1016/j.patter.2023.100779

Key findingWidely used AI-writing detectors misclassified more than half of essays by non-native English writers as "AI-generated" while showing near-perfect accuracy on native speakers' essays — meaning AI-cheating enforcement in schools structurally generates false accusations against English learners and immigrant students.

sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666389923001307

Yoder-Himes, D.R., et al. (2022). Racial, skin tone, and sex disparities in automated proctoring software. Frontiers in Education, 7, 881449. doi:10.3389/feduc.2022.881449

Key findingFirst quantitative audit of a major automated exam-proctoring system: Black students and students with darker skin tones were significantly more likely to be flagged as potential cheaters requiring instructor review, with women with the darkest skin tones flagged most of all.

Buolamwini, J., & Gebru, T. (2018). Gender Shades: Intersectional accuracy disparities in commercial gender classification. Proceedings of Machine Learning Research, 81, 77–91 (Conference on Fairness, Accountability and Transparency).

Key findingFoundational audit of commercial facial-analysis systems: error rates of up to ~35% for darker-skinned women versus under 1% for lighter-skinned men — the upstream bias documented in education-specific systems above.

d. Exploitative Design & Data Extraction Targeting Disadvantaged Children

Radesky, J., et al. (2022) — cross-reference Section 9. Prevalence and characteristics of manipulative design in mobile applications used by children. JAMA Network Open, 5(6), e2217641.

Key findingManipulative "dark pattern" design was most prevalent in apps used by children from lower-socioeconomic-status households; children's attention is exploited for monetization along class lines.

Zhao, F., Egelman, S., Weeks, H.M., Kaciroti, N., Miller, A.L., & Radesky, J.S. (2020) — cross-reference Section 9. Data collection practices of mobile applications played by preschool-aged children. JAMA Pediatrics, 174(12), e203345.

Key findingPervasive third-party data transmission from preschoolers' apps; combined with the CDT findings above, the students whose data is most extracted and acted upon are those with the least power to contest it.

e. Expert Analysis & Reporting

Aspen Tech Policy Hub. EdTech Equity Project.

Key findingPolicy analysis of how racial bias enters edtech across the product lifecycle, from training data to design teams to deployment, and a framework for procurement-stage equity audits districts could adopt.

Tanksley, T. (Dr. Tiera Tanksley). Digital (In)Equity.

Key findingScholarship applying critical race theory to edtech and AI in education, documenting how algorithmic tools surveil, misread, and discipline Black students; her peer-reviewed work on Black students' experiences of AI and "critical race algorithmic literacies" can be added here for academic settings.

The Hechinger Report. "How EdTech Can Worsen Racial Inequality."

Key findingAccessible journalistic synthesis of the gap-widening and bias mechanisms documented in the peer-reviewed sources above.
Taken together (1) the best longitudinal evidence shows technology-first interventions widen achievement gaps (subsection a); (2) the same devices import a surveillance and discipline apparatus that falls hardest on Black, Hispanic, disabled, and LGBTQ+ students (subsection b); (3) the AI tools themselves measurably misjudge Black students' speech, English learners' writing, and darker-skinned students' faces (subsection c); and (4) the commercial design and data practices behind these products disproportionately exploit lower-income children (subsection d). True equity investments are the human and analog defaults with the strongest evidence base: teachers, books, tutoring, and outdoor time.
＊

Evidence Notes

Brain imaging studies (Section 1) are associational and mostly cross-sectional; lead authors themselves note causation is not proven. Framing them as "associations during the most formative window of brain development, warranting precaution in schools" is accurate, and matches the precautionary logic used by Sweden's Karolinska Institutet.
