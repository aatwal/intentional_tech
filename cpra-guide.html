How to request district records under the California Public Records Act (CPRA)

You don't need a lawyer or any special standing to ask SMFCSD for records. Here's how the process actually works, plus a template you can send today.

Sign the petition Explore 11 years of school data

What the CPRA gives you

The California Public Records Act (Government Code section 7920.000 et seq.) gives every person, resident or not, the right to inspect or get copies of most records held by a public agency, including a school district. You don't have to explain why you want the records, and you don't need to be a parent, resident, or taxpayer of the district to ask.

"Records" is interpreted broadly: emails, contracts, spreadsheets, meeting materials, and data exports can all qualify, as long as they relate to the district's business and aren't covered by a specific legal exemption (student privacy records under FERPA, attorney-client communications, and personnel matters are common ones you'll run into).

Before you request

  1. 1 Get specific about what you want. "All emails about technology" will get bounced back for clarification or take months. "Amplify/Desmos and Zearn vendor contracts and invoices from the 2023-24 and 2024-25 school years" is something staff can actually search for.
  2. 2 Decide on a date range and format. Narrowing to a school year or a specific committee, and asking for electronic copies rather than paper, both speed things up.
  3. 3 Send it in writing to the district's public records contact, not to an individual trustee. Check SMFCSD's Superintendent's Office page or call (650) 312-7348 if you're not sure who currently handles records requests.

Email template

California Public Records Act request

This is a formal legal request, not an advocacy email, so keep it factual and specific. You don't have to explain why you want the records.

Subject
California Public Records Act Request
Body
To the San Mateo-Foster City School District Public Records Custodian, Pursuant to the California Public Records Act (Government Code section 7920.000 et seq.), I am requesting copies of the following records: [Describe the records as specifically as you can, e.g. "Aggregate student device usage logs (screen time, app or site categories accessed) for grades TK-8, districtwide, for the 2024-25 and 2025-26 school years" or "All Tech Use Task Force meeting agendas, minutes, and materials from [date range]"] If any portion of this request is denied, please cite the specific statutory exemption justifying the withholding of each record, and provide any reasonably segregable, non-exempt portion of the withheld records, as required under the Act. I understand the District has 10 days from receipt of this request to determine whether it holds disclosable records responsive to this request and to notify me of that determination. If it would be helpful to narrow the scope of this request, please let me know. I would prefer to receive responsive records electronically, by email, if possible. Thank you, [Your name] [Your phone or email]

What happens after you send it

Initial determination
The district has 10 calendar days from receipt to tell you whether it holds records responsive to your request, and roughly when they'll be available (Gov. Code §7922.535).
Possible extension
In "unusual circumstances" (records held off-site, a voluminous request, or a need to consult another department), the district can extend that determination by up to 14 more days, and must tell you it's doing so.
"Promptly available" isn't a fixed deadline
The 10-day (or 24-day) clock is for the district's determination, not for handing over the records themselves. Larger or more complex requests can reasonably take several weeks to actually produce.
Fees
Inspecting records in person is free. The district can charge for the direct cost of duplication if you request copies, but generally can't charge for staff time spent searching or reviewing, with some exceptions for specially programmed data extracts.
Tip: if a request feels like it's stalling, a polite follow-up referencing your original send date and asking for a status update is usually enough to get it moving again.

If your request is denied or delayed

Ask for the specific exemption
A denial has to cite the specific statutory exemption being claimed for each withheld record, not just "not disclosable." If the district doesn't do this, you can ask them to.
Ask for segregable portions
Even if part of a record is legitimately exempt (say, a student's name), the district is still required to release the rest of the record with that portion redacted, rather than withholding the whole thing.
Consider narrowing and resubmitting
Sometimes a denial is really about scope or cost, not the content. A narrower, more specific follow-up request can succeed where a broad one didn't.
Escalation options
If you believe a denial is improper, options include a written appeal to the Superintendent or Board, a complaint to the California Attorney General's Public Records Act Coordinator, or, as a last resort, a petition for a writ of mandate in superior court. These get more formal and time-consuming at each step.
This page is general information for parents, not legal advice. If a request is complex, high-stakes, or you're considering formal escalation, it's worth consulting an attorney familiar with the CPRA.
