Help us ask for two practical changes

We're asking every family to send one email to the Superintendent and School Board. If we all ask for the same two things, in our own words, the district hears one clear, consistent message.

Jump to email templates Sign the petition

Before you send

  1. 1 Send your email to Superintendent Diego Ochoa at diegoochoa@smfcsd.net, and CC all five School Board members so they see how many community members are asking for these changes: Maggie Trinh (President) at maggietrinh@smfcsd.net, Gene Kim (Vice President) at gkim@smfcsd.net, LaTisa Brooks at latisabrooks@smfcsd.net, Alison Proctor at aproctor@smfcsd.net, and Stacey Ho at sho@smfc.k12.ca.us. Contact info is also kept current on the SMFCSD website. Optional: let us know you sent it by emailing smfcsd@intentionaltechinschools.com with "Sent!" That helps us track participation.
  2. 2 Sign with your own name, or "A Concerned SMFCSD Parent," not "Intentional Tech in Schools." Individual voices from many different families carry more weight than one coordinated message, since they show these concerns are broadly shared.
  3. 3 Send it sometime in the next week. Spreading emails out over several days helps them read as genuine, individual messages rather than a single blast, while still giving the district time to act.
  4. 4 Keep it personal. The strongest emails are written in your own words, personal and respectful, and brief: a few paragraphs is plenty. Include the two requests below, one personal experience or observation, and why the changes matter to your family.

The two requests

  • 1Block student access to YouTube on school-issued devices, while continuing to allow teacher-directed educational use.
  • 2Block student access to generative AI tools (e.g. Gemini, ChatGPT) on school-issued devices, while continuing to allow teacher-directed educational use.

Both are practical, cost-free changes the district can make without a lengthy stakeholder process. They preserve technology where it serves a clear instructional purpose while limiting the kinds of open-ended, unsupervised use that don't serve student learning.

Email templates

These are just starting points. Adapt the tone and language to reflect your own voice. The strongest emails are the ones that sound like you.

1. Direct and to the point

Formal opening, leads with the two requests. Good if you want to keep it short.

Subject
Two Practical Technology Changes Before School Starts
Body
Dear Superintendent Ochoa and Members of the School Board, [Optional: add one or two sentences about your own experience or observation here. For example: "We as parents were given zero warning or rules for implementing Chromebooks, contracts, guardrails, etc. I had no idea opting out was an option."] What concerns me most is that children only get one elementary school experience. I want technology to be used when it genuinely improves learning, but I don't want it to replace books, handwriting, discussion, or hands-on learning when those are more effective. I'm respectfully asking the district to implement two practical changes before the start of the school year: • Block student access to YouTube on school-issued devices (while continuing to allow teacher-directed educational use). • Block student access to generative AI tools on school-issued devices (while continuing to allow teacher-directed educational use). These seem like practical, no-cost changes that would better support student learning while preserving technology where it has a clear educational purpose. Thank you for your time and consideration. Sincerely, [Your name]

Leads with your own family's experience. Good if screen time already feels personal to you.

Subject
A Parent's Perspective on Technology Use in SMFCSD
Body
Hello, I'm a parent in SMFCSD, and I really hope the district will reconsider how much technology is being used during the school day. [Optional: add one or two sentences about your own experience or observation here. For example: "We've intentionally delayed giving our child a phone, but it feels like the Chromebook has become one anyway…"] Schools should reinforce the habits many families are trying to build at home, not work against them. I want my child to spend more time reading, writing, discussing ideas, and learning directly from teachers, and less time navigating unnecessary online distractions. 3. Focused on the youngest students

Leans on the case that kids can't be expected to self-regulate around addictive, engagement-driven platforms. Good for any grade level.

Subject
Please Block YouTube on School-Issued Devices
Body
Dear Superintendent Ochoa and Members of the School Board, [Optional: add one or two sentences about your own experience or observation here. For example: "My child's teacher contacted me because he kept getting distracted on his Chromebook during class. What is a parent supposed to do from home? I wish we had the option of not having to use the device in those instances."] Giving young children unrestricted access to highly engaging platforms like YouTube and generative AI chatbots during the school day isn't a reasonable expectation. Children shouldn't be expected to exercise adult levels of self-control while they're still developing those skills. I'm asking the district to make two simple changes before school starts: • Block student access to YouTube on school-issued devices (while continuing to allow teacher-directed educational use). • Block student access to generative AI tools on school-issued devices (while continuing to allow teacher-directed educational use). These seem like common-sense, no-cost changes that would help students focus and better support learning. Thank you for considering them. [Your name]

Personal stories you can borrow

Your email doesn't need a dramatic story. Even a brief observation can be powerful. These are real experiences shared anonymously by parents in our district; borrow one if it matches your own.

  • My child's kindergarten class appeared to spend a surprising amount of time simply getting students logged into their individual accounts before instruction could begin.
  • I was encouraged by teachers who have already reduced classroom screen use. That makes me hopeful the district can establish clearer, more consistent expectations across all schools.
  • I've learned that many students know how to bypass GoGuardian and access websites that are supposed to be restricted.
  • We have been intentional about not allowing our child to have a phone during middle school. It defeats the purpose when they can access many of the same apps, websites, and distractions on their school-issued Chromebook.
  • I worry about what children may be exposed to through other students' device use, even if my own child isn't intentionally seeking it out.
  • I've noticed my child and their school friends talking more about YouTube videos, memes, or online trends that seem to have originated from content viewed on school-issued devices.
  • My child needed glasses at a very young age. While I know many factors affect vision, I worry about the amount of screen exposure children now receive during the school day.
  • Since technology became a routine part of the school day, I've noticed my child becoming more focused on getting through tasks quickly than slowing down to think deeply or work carefully.

Talking points to personalize your email

Mix and match a line or two from whichever angle matches your own view. You don't need to use them all.

I'm the parent of a TK–2 student

  • Young children learn best through books, handwriting, conversation, play, and direct interaction with teachers. Schools should maximize those experiences during these foundational years.
  • The early elementary years are when children build foundational literacy, numeracy, attention, and social skills.
  • The earliest grades should prioritize human interaction and hands-on learning whenever possible.

My biggest concern is YouTube

  • Unrestricted access to YouTube can expose students to distracting or inappropriate content during the school day.
  • Blocking student access to YouTube does not prevent teachers from using educational videos when they support instruction.
  • Even when children begin with educational content, YouTube is designed to keep users engaged through recommendations, autoplay, and short-form videos.
  • School-issued devices should support learning, not entertainment.
  • It's unreasonable to expect students to rely solely on willpower against platforms explicitly engineered to exploit their attention.

I'm concerned about student health

  • Schools have an opportunity to model healthy, intentional technology use.
  • We continue to learn more about the effects of excessive screen exposure on children's attention, sleep, vision, mental health, and executive functioning.
  • Reducing unnecessary screen exposure will help support children's attention, focus, and sustained learning.
  • The school day should maximize opportunities for movement, conversation, hands-on learning, and face-to-face interaction.

I'm concerned about learning outcomes

  • Test scores have declined every year from 2019 to 2025. That doesn't show causation, but it's worth investigating whether heavy investment in classroom technology is helping or hurting.
  • Every instructional choice involves tradeoffs: more screen time often means less time for books, discussion, writing by hand, and hands-on learning.
  • Technology should complement excellent teaching, not replace developmentally appropriate learning experiences.

I'm concerned about equity

  • Every student deserves access to high-quality instruction, not just access to technology.
  • Equity isn't simply about giving every student the same device; it's about giving every student the greatest opportunity to learn and succeed.
  • Students who genuinely need a school device at home should absolutely have that option.
  • Students with attention or executive functioning challenges often have the most to gain from reducing unnecessary digital distractions.
  • We expanded student access to technology in the name of equity, but did we ever verify that routine screen-based learning improves outcomes for everyone?

I'm asking for transparency and accountability

  • Families deserve greater transparency about how school-issued devices are actually being used.
  • Today's technology already allows districts to measure student device use, including non-instructional content during school hours. Many districts already review and report this.
  • Rather than assuming there isn't a problem, let's look at the data we already have. Either way, we should be curious enough to ask.
  • We can't improve what we don't measure. Transparency builds trust.

Speaking at a meeting

How to sign up
In person: submit a speaker card to a Board member or the Assistant Superintendent of Student Services before public comment is called. Virtual: click the "raise hand" icon in Zoom before the Board President calls for speakers.
Time limit
Individual statements are generally limited to three minutes; the Board President can grant more at their discretion.
Non-agenda items
The board can hear your comment but legally can't take action on anything not already posted on the agenda, so public comment is for raising an issue and being heard, not an immediate vote.
