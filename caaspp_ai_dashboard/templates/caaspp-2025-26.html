What stands out

  • The 2026 "facing hardship" figures match CDE's data exactly on all three pages.
  • The "not facing hardship" group is always 0.3–1.1 points higher in the report, in both years. The district may define that group differently from CDE's "not socioeconomically disadvantaged"; the report doesn't say.
  • The 2023 figures are often off by 0.1–0.6 points, which may mean the district used an earlier release of CDE's 2023 results.
Why both groups can improve while the district declines: the report's message is that students facing and not facing economic hardship both gained from 2023 to 2026, and CDE's data agrees. But over the same years the district-wide ELA figure fell from 55.6% to 52.7%, and Math was flat (51.0% to 50.5%). The share of tested students classified as facing hardship rose from about 31% to 45% in that time (in ELA, 2,006 to 2,733 students facing hardship and 4,445 to 3,298 not). If the students who changed groups score below the "not facing hardship" average but above the "facing hardship" average, moving them raises both groups' averages without any student's score changing. Statisticians call this the Will Rogers effect. Until it's clear why so many more students are classified as facing hardship, the per-group gains can't be read as improvement on their own.

We read the report's figures from its charts, since they aren't published as data. A few labels are small, so a misreading of 0.1 is possible.

Both groups up, district down: other districts

At first glance it seems impossible for both groups to improve while the district as a whole gets worse. It happens when students move between the groups. Suppose some students who used to count as not facing hardship are now counted as facing hardship, and their scores fall between the two groups' averages. They were below the "not facing hardship" average, so moving them out raises it. They're above the "facing hardship" average, so moving them in raises that too. Both averages go up, even though no student's score changed. Meanwhile the all-students figure, which doesn't depend on how students are grouped, shows what actually happened.

This doesn't prove the per-group gains aren't real; the overall drop could have other causes, and some real improvement may be mixed in. But while this many students are changing groups, "both groups improved" can't be taken at face value.

To see how common this is, we looked for districts where the share facing hardship rose at least 8 points from 2022–23 to 2025–26 and, in the same subject, both groups' scores improved while the district-wide score fell. About 15 districts fit in at least one subject, including Novato, Davis, Dublin, Glendale, Fremont and Templeton. SMFCSD is one of four that fit in both ELA and Math, with Novato, Davis and Dublin. Its overall ELA drop, from 55.6% to 52.7%, is the largest in the group, while both of its groups rose 3–7 points.

In short: the district's report and the data

  • The report's 2026 figures are accurate. Its "facing hardship" numbers match CDE's data exactly on all three pages we checked.
  • A few figures don't match. The "not facing hardship" group is 0.3–1.1 points higher in the report than in CDE's data, some 2023 figures are off by up to 0.6 points, and the grade 8 Math page has 2023 labels that appear to be copied from another chart.
  • Both groups did improve, but the district overall did not. From 2022–23 to 2025–26, district-wide ELA fell from 55.6% to 52.7% and Math was flat (51.0% to 50.5%), even as both groups' scores rose.
  • The groups changed size a lot. The share of tested students classified as facing hardship rose from 31% to 45%, mostly in 2023–24. Moving students between groups can raise both groups' averages without any student's score changing (the Will Rogers effect).
  • Few districts saw a rise this large. SMFCSD's 14-point increase ranks 20th of about 580 California districts. The typical district's share barely moved.
  • SMFCSD stands out from its neighbors. Several San Mateo County districts spiked in 2023–24 and fell back the next year; SMFCSD's share stayed up and rose again. No other district in the county is up more than about 4 points.
  • The pattern is rare. Of the roughly 15 districts where both groups rose while the overall score fell, SMFCSD is one of four where it happened in both ELA and Math, and it had the largest overall ELA drop.
  • Bottom line: until the district explains why so many more students are now classified as facing hardship, its "both groups improved" message can't be read as improvement on its own. The all-students figures are the most reliable measure of how the district is doing.
